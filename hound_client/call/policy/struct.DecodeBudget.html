Expand description

The live cap on how many remote video tiles may hold a decoder, walked between MIN_DECODE_SESSIONS and the platform’s static ceiling (hound_engine::api::Engine::decode_session_budget) against whether the RT audio mix thread is keeping its schedule. Fed to hound_media::allocate_video’s decode_budget, which does the actual cutting — from the BOTTOM of its priority order, so the tiles that lose their decoder are the ones nobody is watching.

Sans-IO: now is the only clock, so every rule below is unit-tested.

§Halve down, crawl up — the opposite asymmetry to LayerHysteresis

The tile hysteresis restores FAST (a ~200 ms upgrade debounce, no dwell) because an upgrade is the direct answer to a user expanding a tile. This controller is the mirror image: it cuts in one multiplicative step and gives a slot back once a MINUTE.

That is not a second opinion about the same trade-off — it is what makes the first one safe. A decode cut hides tiles, and hiding a tile is a layer-axis move the fast-restore side of LayerHysteresis would otherwise undo within a second of the CPU recovering, at which point the load returns and the cut repeats: a gallery of tiles blinking on a one-second period, forever, on a machine that is genuinely too slow. Slewing k back at a minute per slot means the set of tiles is stable across the whole window in which the hysteresis is free to move individual ones. The cut’s own actuation is still absorbed per tile by the 1.5 s debounce and the 6 s dwell — this controller decides the budget, never the timing of any one subscription.

The price of that asymmetry, stated plainly because it is what makes every safeguard around it load-bearing: the walk down is four passes and the walk back is thirteen minutes (16 → 3 is three halvings inside seven seconds; 3 → 16 is thirteen grants at one a minute). A cut is therefore very nearly irreversible within a single call, which is why the evidence has to be good before one lands — hence DECODE_WARMUP and the calibrated threshold in Self::observe.

§Always a number, never “unbounded”

Self::observe returns a usize and the caller always passes it as Some(k). Handing the allocator None while the budget sits at its ceiling would be simpler by one branch and WRONG: the ceiling is not a controller starting point, it is the platform’s hard limit — on Apple, the number of VideoToolbox sessions that exist before a decoder simply fails to build, with no software fallback behind it. A 32-peer gallery must be cut to 16 on the very first pass, before any evidence has been gathered, so the ceiling has to reach the allocator on every pass including that one. (The cut is a no-op whenever the receiver renders fewer tiles than k, which is the common case and is exactly what allocate_video’s order.truncate(k) already does.)

§A cut must not fund an upgrade

Hiding tiles frees bitrate, and allocate_video’s upgrade phase will spend it on the survivors. That is correct when k is only the platform’s STATIC ceiling — 32 tiles on Apple’s 16 says nothing about this box’s CPU, and a sharper stage tile is exactly what the freed budget should buy. It is wrong the moment the DYNAMIC half has cut (Self::cpu_constrained): the receiver has evidenced that it is short of CPU, and trading 24 thumbnails for one 720p decode is not the saving it asked for. So while k < ceiling the shell folds hound_media::cpu_crisis_cap into every gathered tile’s demand and the upgrade ladder simply stops there. The cap is per-content: a camera keeps its richest below-source rung (360p20 is cheap to decode), while a share is pinned to its 360p15 legibility floor rather than the top of its own ladder — SCREEN_MID_RUNG is 720p30, within a rounding error of the source’s decode cost, so capping a share there would leave the clamp doing nothing for the most expensive leg in the call. A 360p share during a CPU crisis is the lesser evil against a share whose rung is what is starving the mix thread. The clamp lives in the shell so allocate_video stays a pure function of its inputs.

§What this does NOT stabilise: which tiles, only how many

With dominant-speaker ranking live, the allocator’s priority order moves with the MRU speaker list, so at a small k the CUT BOUNDARY walks: whoever drops out of the recent-speakers window swaps places with whoever entered it, and the pair trade decoders. This controller is not the thing that stops that — it fixes the SIZE of the served set, not its membership. What bounds the churn is per tile and downstream: each swap is a layer-axis move, so LayerHysteresis’s debounce and dwell gate it, and the ranker’s own long silence window decides how often the MRU order can move at all. Worth knowing when reading a log of a small-k call: a stable k with tiles trading places is the design, not a bug in it.

The same for the clean run that earns a slot back, re-armed on each grant.

Whether the last pass was TickLate::Unobserved, so the first pass after a pause can restart both clocks rather than bank the interval nothing watched.

§The limitation predicate is self-calibrating

A fixed 3 ms tail would be a load-INDEPENDENT verdict on any platform whose idle lateness already exceeds it. One such platform used to ship: the mix loop paced with a plain thread::sleep, and Windows’ default timer granularity is 15.6 ms, so a perfectly healthy Windows receiver could sit permanently past the threshold and walk every call it joined down to MIN_DECODE_SESSIONS with nothing wrong with it. The engine-side cure has since landed (the mix thread paces off an absolute deadline and holds timeBeginPeriod(1) for the life of a call — audio::mix), which lowers the BASELINE this calibrates against; it does not remove the need to calibrate, because the idle tail is still a per-platform, per-box quantity and a raw threshold would still be a statement about someone’s clock rather than their load.

So the threshold is max(DECODE_TICK_LATE_LIMIT_US, 2 × baseline), where the baseline is the worst p95 seen during DECODE_WARMUP. A clean platform baselines near zero and keeps the absolute 3 ms figure; a 15.6 ms-granularity platform has to double its own idle tail before anything is cut, which is a statement about LOAD rather than about the clock. Taking the warmup MAXIMUM biases the baseline high — a join storm inside the window inflates it — and that is the deliberate direction: an inflated baseline makes this controller quiet, where a deflated one makes it hide people. It is per-establish, so a reconnect re-calibrates.
