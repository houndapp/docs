Dominant-speaker ranking — the receiver-side half of Jitsi’s Last-N, as a pure reducer over a coarse per-peer speaking-level series.

§What this is for

A gallery receiver prices every tile from its rendered size (crate::layer). Size alone cannot know that the 320×180 stamp in the corner is the person currently talking, so a large call spends the same 130 kbps on the speaker as on the 31 people listening. Ranking is the missing signal: the top of the ranking buys a richer rung, the bottom (observed silent, in a crowded call) buys a cheaper one, and the moq subscription for the dominant speaker’s video is opened in a higher priority band. Jitsi measured 45% less CPU and 63% less bandwidth for a 30-participant conference forwarding 10 endpoints on exactly this lever (Grozev et al., Last N, NOSSDAV 2015).

§The signal, and why it is not RTP audio levels

Jitsi’s DominantSpeakerIdentification reads RFC 6464 audio levels (0–127) off every RTP packet inside the SFU. hound’s SFU is codec-blind and forward-no- transcode — it must never look inside media — so the level comes from the sender, which already computes it for its own mic meter, and rides the control plane as a coarse subband at REPORT_INTERVAL_US. Every receiver therefore ranks from the same numbers and independently reaches the same ranking, which is what lets a producer’s shared low layer be sized for “the speaker” without anyone negotiating.

§The anti-flap, and how faithfully it is Jitsi’s

Taken verbatim from DominantSpeakerIdentification: the 300 ms decision cadence, the 13 subbands, the three history windows (5 / 10 / longs), and the three-timescale AND with its constants C1=3 ∧ C2=2 ∧ C3=0 — you must be loud now, recently, and sustained to take dominance from the incumbent. Plus the standard minimum dwell before a switch commits (SWITCH_DWELL_US), which is also Meta’s US10079994B2 refinement (weight dominance by time since the last switch).

Adapted, deliberately and named here so nobody mistakes it for the original:

  • Jitsi’s input is a 20 ms RTP level series and its “relative speech activity” is a log-likelihood ratio over per-subband histograms. Ours is one RMS-derived subband per 300 ms, so a histogram over 13 bands of a single sample carries no information. The per-window quantity is instead the count of ACTIVE samples (subband ≥ SPEECH_SUBBAND) in the window, and C1/C2/C3 gate the challenger’s margin over the incumbent rather than a log ratio. The shape — three timescales combined with AND, with a wider margin required on the shortest — is preserved exactly; the units are not the same units.
  • Jitsi’s “assume no levels after 40 ms idle” becomes IDLE_US (2 s), because our reports are 300 ms apart and are suppressed while a peer is silent (a silent peer sends one trailing zero and then nothing — see the emitter).
  • The long window is 30 samples (9 s); Jitsi’s “longs” have no published count, only the ratio of medium-to-long aggregation.

§Determinism

No clock, no IO, no floats in the decision: SpeakerRanking::decide takes now_us and materializes the sample grid itself, so a test drives ten minutes of call in a loop and the ranking is a pure function of the reports it was fed. Peers are held in a BTreeMap so ties break identically on every machine.

Enums§

SpeakerTier
Where a peer sits in the ranking, and the only thing policy is allowed to read. The four cases are deliberately not an ordering: Unknown and Silent are different — the first is a peer we have no evidence about (an un-upgraded client that never reports, a peer that just joined), the second is a peer we have watched be quiet for the whole long window. Only the second may be downgraded, for the same reason TileDemand trusts a reported hide but bounds an inferred one.

DOMINANT_K
How many of the ranked speakers are treated as “the people who matter” — the dominant plus the two who spoke before them. Three is the industry’s answer everywhere it is visible: Jitsi’s loudest.num-loudest = 3, and Google Meet creating exactly three receive-only audio m-lines.
IDLE_US
A peer with no report for this long is read as silent from then on. Jitsi’s equivalent is 40 ms, sized to a 20 ms RTP level cadence; ours is sized to a 300 ms report cadence that is suppressed while silent (a silent peer sends one trailing zero and then nothing at all, so the absence of reports is the steady state, not evidence of a lost peer). Six missed intervals is unambiguous.

History windows, in 300 ms samples: 1.5 s / 3 s / 9 s. The first two counts are Jitsi’s (5 immediates, 10 mediums).

MAX_CATCHUP_SAMPLES 🔒
Ceiling on the sample grid a single SpeakerRanking::decide will materialize. A call that was suspended (a backgrounded app, a debugger breakpoint) resumes with an arbitrarily large gap; filling it sample by sample would be unbounded work for a series that is entirely silence anyway. Past this the grid is reset to now, which reads as “no history”, the safe state.

RECENT_SPEAKERS
How many past dominant speakers stay ranked. Jitsi’s speech-activity.recent-speakers-count = 10.
REPORT_INTERVAL_US
The decision cadence, and the reporting cadence that feeds it — DominantSpeakerIdentification re-decides every 300 ms.
SPEECH_SUBBAND
The subband at or above which a sample counts as somebody speaking.
SUBBANDS
How many coarse levels the wire carries. Jitsi buckets RFC 6464’s 0–127 into 13 subbands for exactly this decision; the same 13 buckets over our 0.0..=1.0 meter level make the wire byte a stable, flap-resistant quantization (a level jittering by ±3% cannot change the reported band).
SWITCH_DWELL_US
Minimum dwell on a dominant speaker before another may take over. The three-timescale AND already rejects an interjection; this bounds the switch rate when two people genuinely alternate — the same fast-down/slow-up spirit as LayerHysteresis, and the mechanism Meta’s US10079994B2 describes as weighting dominance by time since the last switch.
